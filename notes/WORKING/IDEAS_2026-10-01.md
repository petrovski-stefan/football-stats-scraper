**Phase 2 — The preregistered study**

**Bar:** keep the **top 20%** of fixtures by model score. The comeback rate of that slate must have a **Wilson 95% lower bound above the pooled base rate**, on leagues and seasons that were not used to build the rule. At ~25,000 matches, a 20% slate is ~5,000 fixtures. So even a 1.2× lift (5.5% → 6.6%) passes it clearly.

**Two independent splits, both required:**

- *walk-forward in time*: an expanding window, as `walk_forward` of the old `modelling/experiment.py` did (git `633ff90`);
- *leave-one-league-out*: the claim is that the filter works in other leagues, and a league holdout is the only test of that. It also takes the place of the clean holdout that the project no longer has (2025-26 was opened three times).

**Models:** the generative scoreline model of the old `modelling/scoreline.py` (git `633ff90`), plus a direct classifier on P(HT decisive ∧ FT decisive ∧ ≥3 goals).

**Always reported:**

- the retained-rate curve (keep the top 5%–65%), with CIs;
- the oracle ceiling, calculated again on the new corpus;
- **headroom recovered** = (achieved − base) / (ceiling − base). It is the main number, whatever the result;
- the AUC per component. In the 2026-07-25 study, that AUC found the cause of the failure.

### 24 — Relationship discovery (a 13-step method, 2026-10-16)

**Source:** a 13-step method that the user pasted on 2026-10-16. It finds which features, alone or together, relate to a yes/no target. It was written for a general table of 40,000 rows × 500 columns. Our table: 36,238 matches × about 180 pre-match columns. The targets: 1,891 comebacks (5.22%) and 812 matches with 7+ goals (2.24%).

**The split (the user, 2026-10-16):** the non-ML methods go into `analysis/`, with their docs in `notes/ANALYSIS/`. The ML methods go into a later package with the same tree as `analysis/`. That package is not for now. **The line:** a method is ML when it needs a fitted predictive model whose settings are tuned on the data. Counts, tests, regressions without tuning, GAMs and information measures are non-ML.

#### The ML methods (later, in the ML package)

| step | method | why it is ML |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | sparse interaction selection (for example a lasso over interaction terms) | its penalty is tuned on the data |
| 6 | subgroups taken from a decision tree | a fitted, tuned tree |
| 6 | H-statistics (interaction strength) | they need the partial dependence of a fitted model |
| 6 | partial dependence summaries | they need a fitted model |
| 10 | nested resampling | it tunes a model inside each resample |

#### The 13 steps (every other method is non-ML, for `analysis/`)

1. **Set the objective and the guardrails.** Output: a discovery spec with measurable acceptance criteria.
   - Define the positive class: what "yes" means.
   - Select the goal: association, explanation or causal inference. An association alone does not show a cause.
   - Set the smallest effect that has practical value.
   - Set the largest interaction order N (2–5 at first).
   - Decide if the target is imbalanced, and if some subgroups are more important.
   - Set the acceptable false-discovery rate and the smallest subgroup size.
2. **Profile and validate the data.** Output: a clean analysis table, feature metadata and a data-quality report.
   - Find missing values, duplicates, invalid values and inconsistent categories.
   - Find constant and near-constant columns.
   - Find identifiers, leakage columns and columns that are known only after the outcome.
   - Give each feature a type: binary, categorical, ordinal, continuous, date/time or identifier.
   - Examine the class balance, the missing values by target, and the distributions.
   - Find correlated or redundant columns.
   - Methods: data profiling, schema validation, missing-value analysis, cardinality checks, correlation matrices and leakage audits.
3. **Analyse each feature alone.** Output: a ranked table of feature associations, with effect sizes, uncertainty and adjusted significance. Do not rank by p-value only: in a large table, a very small effect can be significant.
   - Binary and categorical features: target rates by category, contingency tables, the chi-square test, and Fisher's exact test where it applies.
   - Continuous features: target-conditioned distributions, standardized differences, rank-based tests and logistic-regression coefficients.
   - Ordinal features: trend tests and monotonic association measures, where they apply.
   - Effect sizes: risk difference, odds ratio, relative risk, or a suitable effect size for a continuous feature.
   - Give the confidence intervals, the sample sizes and the target prevalence.
4. **Find relations between pairs.** Output: a ranked list of candidate pairs, with joint effect sizes, support counts and evidence of interaction.
   - Categorical × categorical: stratified contingency tables, log-linear analysis, and target rates across joint categories.
   - Numeric × numeric: interaction effects, conditional target rates, and two-dimensional bins where they are justified.
   - Numeric × categorical: target associations across the groups, and the change of the numeric effect between groups.
   - Any pair: logistic regression with interaction terms (X1 × X2).
   - Screening: mutual information, then an effect-size check and a statistical check.
   - Check if the pair adds information beyond its two features. A pair can be useful for one category of a third feature only.
   - The method checks every pair: 124,750 for 500 columns. For us, about 16,000.
5. **Search for interactions of three or more features**, without a check of every combination. Output: candidate rules, for example A = X AND B > t AND C = Z, with support, target rate and effect size.
   - Beam search: expand good pairs into triplets, and good triplets into larger combinations.
   - Frequent-itemset mining (FP-Growth or Apriori) for categorical conditions, with a target-aware check of each subgroup.
   - Subgroup discovery: readable rules for groups with an unusually high or low target rate.
   - Rule mining: candidate conditions, checked for target enrichment, with guards against rare, misleading rules.
   - Conditional mutual information: features that add information about the target after the conditions that are already in the rule.
   - Hierarchical interaction tests. (Sparse interaction selection is ML: see above.)
   - Use a minimum support, a maximum depth, a computing budget and redundancy pruning.
   - Critical: a strong association in a subgroup is not always an interaction. Test if the combination adds information beyond the main effects.
6. **Find non-linear relations and hidden subgroups.** Output: candidate non-linear effects, thresholds and subgroups for a formal test.
   - Generalized additive models (GAMs) for non-linear relations between a feature and the target.
   - Conditional dependence analysis for relations that change between groups.
   - Target-rate heatmaps, as descriptions only: correlated features can make them mislead.
   - (Decision-tree subgroups, H-statistics and partial dependence are ML: see above.)
7. **Measure the effect and its practical value.** Output: one standard effect profile for each candidate. For each feature or combination, calculate:
   - the support: the number and percent of rows that meet the condition;
   - the target rate P(Y=1 | condition), and the baseline rate P(Y=1);
   - the risk difference (subgroup rate − baseline), and the relative risk or the odds ratio;
   - the confidence intervals;
   - the incremental association: what the combination adds to its main effects;
   - the stability across data splits and populations.
   - Example: an overall rate of 10% and a subgroup rate of 25% give a risk difference of +15 points and a relative risk of 2.5.
8. **Correct for multiple tests.** Output: candidates with adjusted p-values or q-values, and the written test procedure.
   - Benjamini–Hochberg (false-discovery rate) for a large exploratory screen.
   - A stricter family-wise control where a false positive costs much.
   - Count the full search, not only the final list.
   - Permutation tests that keep the group or time structure, where they apply.
   - A plain p-value is not valid after a large search that is not counted.
   - The correction does not remove selection bias, dependence, or overfitting from repeated searches of the same data.
9. **Remove redundant and misleading results.** Output: a short set of distinct relations, not hundreds of repeats.
   - Cluster highly correlated features and near-duplicate rules.
   - Remove a rule that is a strict subset of a stronger, clearer rule, when it adds nothing.
   - Compare each combination with its single features and with simpler combinations.
   - Look for Simpson's paradox, confounding and reversals in subgroups.
   - Flag small subgroups, features with very many categories, and rules that come from few observations.
   - Check if a relation disappears after control for obvious confounders.
10. **Validate on independent data.** Output: a validated catalog that keeps exploratory and confirmed results apart.
    - Keep a holdout, or split the data into discovery and validation (the method's example: 70% and 30%, stratified).
    - Find the candidate rules on the discovery part only.
    - Freeze the rules, the thresholds and the procedures.
    - Evaluate them on the untouched validation data. Calculate the support, rates, effect sizes and intervals again.
    - Confirm the direction and the practical size of each effect.
    - Data in time: validate on a later period. Data in groups: split at the group level.
    - Rare patterns: repeated discovery and validation, with no repeated tuning on the final holdout. (Nested resampling is ML: see above.)
11. **Check robustness and sensitivity.** Output: a robustness score and the known limits of each result.
    - Repeat with other random seeds or resamples.
    - Change the minimum support and the search depth within reason.
    - Compare other definitions of the effect size.
    - Check the effect of the missing-value handling and of outliers.
    - Check the stability across time, regions, segments and other cohorts.
    - Label each rule: robust, true for one population only, or exploratory.
12. **Make a ranked catalog of relations.** Output: a catalog that people can search, with versions, summary tables, rule plots and a dataset to export. Each record has:
    - an id and the feature names;
    - the exact rule: operators, thresholds and category values;
    - the interaction order (1, 2, 3, 4 or more);
    - the support count and percent;
    - the overall and the subgroup target rates;
    - the effect size and its interval;
    - the adjusted significance;
    - the value beyond simpler relations;
    - the validation effect and the stability;
    - the redundancy group, the caveats and the interpretation.
13. **Make it repeatable, monitor it, and run it again.** Output: a pipeline that people can repeat and audit, with lineage and a history of results.
    - Give versions to the datasets, schemas, feature definitions, code and parameters.
    - Record the run time, the memory, the candidate counts and the patterns that were removed.
    - Cache the intermediate results that are used again.
    - Make the search deterministic where possible.
    - Log each tested hypothesis, or keep an auditable record of the search.
    - Run again when new data arrives or distributions change.
    - Alert when a strong relation becomes weaker, or when a new pattern appears.
    - Get an expert review before a high-impact result is used.

#### Changes for our data (Claude, 2026-10-16)

1. **The baseline is the odds.** The method compares a subgroup with P(Y=1). Here, the opening odds hold most of the signal: on 2026-10-09, 0 of 29 stage-3 fields added anything beyond them. So each rate becomes observed ÷ expected under the odds yardstick (`notes/ANALYSIS/SCHEDULE/WHOLE_CORPUS/ALL/ODDS_YARDSTICK.md`), and each regression carries the odds as an offset. The yardstick comes first.
2. **Power sets the limits.** In a search of 10,000 tests, a subgroup needs about 2,262 matches (6.2%) to show a 1.5× lift in comebacks, and 594 (1.6%) for a 2× lift. For 7+ goals: 5,454 (15.1%) and 1,439 (4.0%). So N = 2, and 3 only by beam search on subgroups of 2,000 matches or more. For 7+ goals: pairs at most.
3. **Validation by time and by group.** Discover on the 2024 cohort (17,757 matches), check on the 2025 cohort (18,481), and confirm once on 2026-27 and the calendar-year 2026 seasons. Only these are untouched: earlier scans looked at both cohorts. Permutations and intervals keep each league-season together.
4. **Leakage:** the audit of step 2 is the timing and role of each field in `notes/FIELDS/`, plus the stage-3 leak test. The 15 outcome columns are never features.
5. **Betting value** needs the comeback's own price (HT/FT odds), which nowgoal does not show.
